In the spring of 1799, a nineteen-year-old chieftain named Ranjit Singh made a bold move that would change the destiny of the Punjab forever. Riding into the ancient city of Lahore, he captured it from the Sikh triumvirate that had ruled it for decades. Far from facing resistance, he was welcomed by the local dignitaries and townspeople. Two years later, on April 12, 1801, he was formally crowned Maharaja of Punjab by Sahib Singh Bedi, a direct descendant of Guru Nanak. This momentous coronation marked the official birth of the Sikh Empire, known in Punjabi as the *Sarkar-i-Khalsa* (and frequently called the Lahore Darbar or Lahore State by contemporary Europeans).

To understand how this powerful empire rose, one has to look back at the turbulent history of the Punjab region. Sikhism had begun in the 16th century as a peaceful spiritual path founded by Guru Nanak. However, centuries of fierce conflicts with the Mughal Empire—particularly after the executions of Guru Arjan and Guru Tegh Bahadur—transformed the followers of the faith into a formidable military force. Following the death of the Mughal Emperor Aurangzeb in 1707, the tenth guru, Guru Gobind Singh, established a dedicated militia known as the Khalsa Fauj. By the mid-18th century, after waves of invasions by Afghan rulers like Ahmad Shah Abdali, the Sikhs had organized themselves into a confederation of twelve independent military states called *misls*. 

Ranjit Singh was born into the ruling family of one of these, the Sukerchakia misl, in 1780. Succeeding his father at just thirteen years old in 1792, he quickly proved his mettle. After securing Lahore, he embarked on a relentless campaign to unite all the fractured Sikh misls under his single authority, a process that was largely complete by 1813. 

With his home base secure, the Maharaja turned his attention outward, systematically expelling Afghan influence from the region. In 1813, his general Mohkam Chand captured the strategic Attock Fort. Five years later, in 1818, the conquest of Multan completely shattered Afghan power in the Punjab. The following year, Ranjit Singh’s forces swept across the foothills of the Himalayas to defeat the Afghans at the Battle of Shopian, annexing the breathtaking Kashmir Valley. Figures like the brilliant general Hari Singh Nalwa secured legendary victories in Hazara and Peshawar, the latter of which was formally annexed in 1834. 

Meanwhile, to the east, the rising power of the British East India Company prompted a strategic boundary agreement. In 1809, the Treaty of Amritsar was signed, establishing the Sutlej River as the eastern border between the British territories and the Sikh Empire, securing a long period of peaceful coexistence on that front.

While Ranjit Singh modernized his army with cutting-edge European-style training, weapons, and heavy artillery, his tributary nobles were busy expanding the empire’s borders in other directions. Gulab Singh Dogra, a loyal noble, restored his family’s estates in Jammu and sent his daring general, Zorawar Singh, further north. Together, they brought the high-altitude kingdoms of Ladakh and Baltistan under Sikh suzerainty in the late 1830s. Even a brief, daring invasion of Tibet was launched during the Sino-Sikh War in 1841. 

At its mid-19th-century peak, the Sikh Empire was a magnificent, religiously diverse realm. Stretching from Gilgit and Tibet in the north to the deserts of Sindh in the south, and from the Khyber Pass in the west to the Sutlej River in the east, it spanned eight distinct provinces and housed an estimated population of 12 million people, making it the 12th most populous state in the world at the time. 

Yet, the brilliant structure built by the Maharaja rested heavily on his personal leadership. When Maharaja Ranjit Singh passed away in 1839, the empire was suddenly left without its master tactician. Internal divisions, political chaos, and court assassinations quickly fractured the once-mighty Lahore Darbar. Sensing vulnerability, the British East India Company aggressively stoked these internal conflicts. The inevitable clash followed in the Anglo-Sikh Wars, and after a fierce struggle, the Sikh Empire was defeated. In 1849, the state was formally dissolved and annexed by the British—falling into history as the very last major region of the Indian subcontinent to be conquered.
