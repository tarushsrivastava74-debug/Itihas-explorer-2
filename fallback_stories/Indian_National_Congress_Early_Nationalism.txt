On the damp, breezy morning of December 28, 1885, inside the hall of the Gokuldas Tejpal Sanskrit College in Bombay, seventy-two men sat quietly, waiting for history to begin. They were mostly lawyers, journalists, and intellectuals from every corner of India, brought together by a shared dream. Among them sat prominent figures like Pherozeshah Mehta and Dinshaw Wacha, alongside a retired British civil servant named Allan Octavian Hume. Hume had spent months writing letters and rallying support, believing that educated Indians deserved a formal platform to voice their grievances to the British Raj. That morning, presided over by Umesh Chandra Banerjee, the Indian National Congress was officially born.

In those early years, the Congress was a polite and orderly assembly. They weren't calling for complete independence just yet; instead, they passed resolutions asking for civil service reforms, the expansion of legislative councils, and solutions to the country's economic struggles. A towering figure of this early era was Dadabhai Naoroji, affectionately known as the Grand Old Man of India, who later became the first Indian Member of the British Parliament, carrying the nation's struggles straight to the heart of London.

As decades rolled on, the polite discussions of the elite transformed into a mass movement. Following the pivotal shift after 1920 under the leadership of Mahatma Gandhi, the Congress stepped out of elite conference halls and onto the dusty streets, leading millions of everyday Indians in the relentless fight for freedom. When midnight struck on August 15, 1947, and India finally broke the chains of colonial rule, the Congress party stepped naturally into the role of nation-builders.

Jawaharlal Nehru, India’s first Prime Minister, led the party and the country into a brand-new era. Under his vision, the Congress championed a secular state, a mixed economy, and the famous Five-Year Plans driven by the Planning Commission. For the first fifty years after independence, the party dominated the Indian political landscape, winning successive elections and holding power at the center for decades.

Yet, ruling a vast and diverse democracy is never simple, and the Congress soon faced its own internal storms. After Nehru and a brief tenure by Lal Bahadur Shastri, Nehru’s daughter, Indira Gandhi, assumed leadership. Her tenure was marked by immense triumphs, such as the sweeping victory in 1971, but also by deep political fractures. These included a major party split in 1969 and the dark, turbulent years of the Emergency from 1975 to 1977, which tested the nation's democratic resilience. 

The decades that followed saw the party experience a continuous cycle of reinvention and electoral shifts. After Indira's tragic passing, her son Rajiv Gandhi led the party to a massive electoral victory in 1984, though defeats soon followed. In 1991, under the leadership of Prime Minister P.V. Narasimha Rao, the party boldly pivoted toward an economic agenda of liberalisation, privatisation, and globalisation—a sharp departure from its previous socialist policies. Meanwhile, the Bharatiya Janata Party (BJP) steadily emerged as its primary national rival.

Entering the twenty-first century, the Congress adapted to the era of coalition politics. Led by Manmohan Singh, the United Progressive Alliance (UPA) won consecutive terms, governing from 2004 to 2014. Singh became the first prime minister since Indira Gandhi to complete a full five-year term and win re-election. However, the political winds shifted dramatically in the 2014 and 2019 general elections, where the party faced heavy defeats, shrinking its footprint in the Lok Sabha.

Despite these setbacks, the journey of the Congress continued to evolve. In the 2024 general elections, under the leadership of figures like Mallikarjun Kharge, the party performed better than expected, winning 99 seats and reclaiming its position as the official opposition in Parliament with its highest seat count in a decade. 

From a modest gathering of 72 men in a Bombay college in 1885 to a modern political force spanning nearly 140 years, the Indian National Congress has woven itself deeply into the fabric of Indian history. It remains a testament to how a simple platform for civic dialogue can grow into a foundational pillar that shapes the destiny of a billion people.
