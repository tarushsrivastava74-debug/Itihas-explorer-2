In 1645, a young Maratha aristocrat named Shivaji scaled the stone walls of the Torna fort, capturing it from the Bijapur Sultanate and setting in motion a historic revolution. Shivaji wasn’t just seizing territory; he was building *Hindavi Swarajya*, meaning self-rule for the people. Operating from the rugged western Deccan Plateau, he relied on brilliant guerrilla tactics to outsmart massive rival armies. By 1674, he was crowned Chhatrapati, or sovereign, of a newly forged Maratha Kingdom. At the time of his death, his realm boasted a formidable network of 300 forts, a disciplined cavalry, and naval establishments guarding the west coast.

Shivaji’s fierce defiance infuriated the mighty Mughal Empire. After his passing, his son Sambhaji took command, successfully fighting off regional rivals. But in 1681, the Mughal Emperor Aurangzeb marched south with an imperial army of 500,000 troops, determined to crush the Maratha insurgency once and for all. For years, Sambhaji held the line. Then, tragedy struck in early 1689. While meeting with his commanders at Sangameshwar, Sambhaji was ambushed and captured due to a betrayal. He was taken by the imperial army and executed, a brutal blow meant to break the spirit of the Marathas. 

Instead, the Maratha resistance only grew more ferocious. Sambhaji’s half-brother Rajaram, and later Rajaram’s widow Tarabai, kept the flame alive by launching relentless raids from hidden strongholds. When Aurangzeb finally died in 1707, Shivaji’s grandson Shahu was released from Mughal captivity. Shahu’s return sparked a brief civil war for the throne against Tarabai, but it also ushered in a golden era of governance. Shahu appointed a sharp administrator named Balaji Vishwanath as his Peshwa, or prime minister. Balaji brilliantly secured Mughal recognition of Shahu as the rightful Chhatrapati.

After Balaji, the office of the Peshwa became hereditary under the Bhat family, wielding immense political authority. Under the legendary military leadership of Peshwa Bajirao I, the Maratha realm exploded in size. Maratha horsemen thundered across the subcontinent. Shahu and his fierce generals—such as Raghoji Bhonsle, Malharrao Holkar, Ranoji Scindia, and the Gaekwads—expanded the borders north to Gwalior, east to Orissa, and west into Gujarat. By the mid-18th century, the Marathas controlled about a third of the entire Indian subcontinent. Unlike the centralized Mughals, the Maratha state operated as a dynamic confederacy of powerful regional families whose power dynamics shifted fluidly from decade to decade.

Yet, such rapid expansion brought formidable rivals. In 1761, the Maratha march to dominance faced a catastrophic wall at the Third Battle of Panipat, where they suffered a devastating defeat at the hands of the Durrani Empire. It was a crushing loss of life and territory. However, the Maratha spirit proved remarkably resilient. Just ten years later, under the brilliant stewardship of Peshwa Madhavrao I, the Marathas aggressively retook most of their lost territories, proving their enduring military strength.

Sadly, Madhavrao’s death marked a turning point. Without his strong central authority, the independent chiefs—the Scindias, Holkars, Bhonsles, and Gaekwads—began turning on one another. By 1802, the Holkar dynasty defeated the reigning Peshwa, Baji Rao II, forcing him into a fatal political miscalculation. Desperate to regain his throne, Baji Rao II turned to the British East India Company for protection. 

This intervention proved disastrous. The British eagerly stepped into the internal feuds, systematically dismantling the fractured confederacy through the Second and Third Anglo-Maratha Wars. By 1818, the great Maratha polity had fallen to the British East India Company, bringing a dramatic and permanent close to an era that had once completely reshaped the destiny of the Indian subcontinent.
