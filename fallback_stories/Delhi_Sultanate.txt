In the year 1206, a sudden assassination in the Indus region reshaped the destiny of the Indian subcontinent. Mu'izz ad-Din Muhammad Ghori—the Ghurid conqueror who had famously defeated the Rajput Confederacy led by Prithviraj Chauhan at the Battle of Tarain years earlier—was killed. With no clear central heir, his vast territories in India were divided among his loyal Turkic military slaves, known as Mamluks. Among them was Qutb ud-Din Aibak, a man whose rise from slavery to supreme command marked the birth of the Delhi Sultanate.

Aibak became the first Sultan of Delhi, initiating the Mamluk dynasty (1206–1290). Though his reign lasted only four years, he laid the groundwork for an empire that would span over three centuries. After his brief tenure and a quick power struggle, his brilliant son-in-law, Shams ud-Din Iltutmish, took the throne. Iltutmish faced immense pressure from rival nobles and neighboring rulers, but through calculated conquests—capturing Multan, Bengal, Ranthambore, and defeating rival claimants—he consolidated the infant empire. 

Following Iltutmish's death in 1236, the sultanate entered a turbulent era of weak rulers and court intrigue. Notably, this period saw the brief, courageous reign of Razia Sultana, one of the very few female rulers in Islamic history, who bravely fought to assert her authority before succumbing to political opposition. Eventually, stability returned under Ghiyas ud-Din Balban. Ruling from 1266 to 1287, Balban ruthlessly crushed the "Corps of Forty," a powerful council of Turkic slave-nobles who had long acted as kingmakers, centralizing absolute power in the hands of the sultan.

The Mamluk dynasty eventually gave way to the Khalji dynasty (1290–1320) when Jalal ud-Din Firuz Khalji seized power. The Khaljis, followed closely by the Tughlaqs (1320–1414), ushered in an aggressive wave of Muslim conquests that plunged deep into South India. Under the Tughlaq dynasty, particularly during the reign of Muhammad bin Tughluq, the Delhi Sultanate reached its absolute geographical zenith, stretching across nearly the entire Indian subcontinent—from modern-day Pakistan and northern India down to the south, and parts of Bangladesh and Nepal.

However, empire-building is rarely a straight line of continuous growth. A catastrophic blow struck northern India in 1398 when the Central Asian conqueror Timur launched a devastating raid on Delhi, leaving the city shattered. This chaos triggered a major political transformation. As the central authority in Delhi weakened, regional powers re-emerged. Hindu kingdoms like the Vijayanagara Empire and the Kingdom of Mewar asserted their independence, alongside powerful new Muslim sultanates in Bengal and the Deccan, such as the Bahmani Sultanate. 

Delhi limped through the 15th century under two more weak or transitional dynasties: the Sayyids (1414–1451) and the Lodis (1451–1526). During these centuries, despite political fragmentation and wars, profound cultural transformations were quietly taking root. The influx of fleeing soldiers, artists, traders, and scholars escaping Mongol raids in Central and West Asia transformed the region. This rich cultural exchange birthed the Hindustani language and magnificent Indo-Islamic architecture. The sultans generally ruled as paramount powers within traditional political frameworks, employing Hindu officials and native converts, while successfully repelling multiple fierce Mongol invasions from the Chagatai Khanate.

The final chapter of the Delhi Sultanate closed in the spring of 1526. Babur, a descendant of Timur from Central Asia, marched into northern India. In a decisive clash, Babur defeated the Lodi forces, bringing an end to the Delhi Sultanate. Yet, the foundations laid over three hundred years did not vanish; instead, they paved the way for a brand-new imperial era under the rising banner of the Mughal Empire.
